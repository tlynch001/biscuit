# Biscuit storytelling

Write for an attentive listener, not a distracted scroller. Use clear visual
storytelling with occasional literary elevation. Earn emotion through action
and concrete detail. Allow a few exceptional lines to carry more weight than
a constant stream of poetic language.

This file is the creative specification for Biscuit stories. Phase 1 does not
generate prose (the `template` story provider copies authored beats). A future
LLM story provider should load this guide. Human authors should too.

Visual continuity — reference assets, human approval, and how shot prompts
use an approved library — is documented in [`ART_DIRECTION.md`](ART_DIRECTION.md).
Prose rules stay here. The picture department lives there.

## Quality benchmark, not a template

`stories/biscuit_in_the_snow.yaml` is Episode One and the benchmark for **care
and emotional effectiveness**. Do not rewrite it. Do not recycle its snow,
rescue structure, courthouse, veteran, metaphors, sentence constructions, or
ending. We want the same level of writing, not the same story again.

## Take the premise seriously

The narrator never winks.

Even when the premise is sentimental or strange, treat the characters and
events sincerely. Avoid jokes about the premise, ironic narration, self-aware
AI language, and explaining that something is emotional. Let feeling arise
from what happens.

## Biscuit remains an ordinary dog

Biscuit does not speak. He does not have human internal monologues. He does
not understand abstract concepts in human language.

His character is expressed through observation, movement, hesitation,
persistence, instinct, and physical contact with people and places. Narration
may interpret his behavior poetically. Biscuit himself stays recognizably a
dog.

## Concrete details before abstraction

The strongest moments begin with things a viewer can see and illustrate: snow
on a coat, a hand, ice on a wheel, warm light, someone kneeling, a dog who
does not leave.

Emotion should usually come from action and physical detail, not from the
narrator declaring sadness, courage, kindness, or loneliness.

## Earn the poetic lines

Plain sentence. Concrete detail. Action. Space. Then, occasionally, a line
that lands.

We want a few memorable literary lines of that caliber. We do not want every
sentence attempting to be profound. Do not turn the narration into a
continuous sequence of metaphors.

## Slightly less density

This prose will be **heard once**, not silently reread.

Keep the literary quality. Give the listener more room: about 15–25% less
syntactic density, not less intelligence or feeling.

Mix sentence lengths. Prefer a rhythm of short observation, short action,
concrete visual detail, then a longer literary sentence, then a pause, then
action. Use an occasional very short sentence at a turning point.

## Restraint

Sentiment is welcome. Sentimentality must be earned.

Avoid common generated-prose habits:

- every paragraph ending on a profound observation
- excessive em dashes
- repeated “not X, but Y” constructions
- endless three-item rhetorical lists
- constant metaphors
- explaining the theme
- telling the audience what to feel
- repeating emotional information
- generic inspirational language
- clichés about hope, destiny, courage, or angels

## Leave some things unexplained

Do not resolve every mystery or explain every motivation. Trust the audience.
This matters even more as Biscuit stories enter other genres.

## Genre should change the world, not Biscuit

Biscuit may appear in radically different literary universes: Victorian
supernatural, American gothic, noir, mystery, adventure.

The selected genre should change the world, rhythm, and imagery. A Victorian
ghost story should feel restrained, eerie, melancholy, and period-appropriate.
Noir should not be the same sentimental template with different nouns.

Biscuit remains the recognizable emotional anchor: same dog, different
universe.

## Strong endings

End on an image, an action, a realization, or a restrained literary
observation that leaves an aftertaste.

Avoid summarizing the moral, “and from that day forward,” explaining what the
audience should have learned, and generic inspirational conclusions. Let the
final imagery carry the meaning. The assembled video holds that last image
after the narrator stops speaking; write an ending that can bear that silence.
